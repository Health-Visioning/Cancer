How many extra cancer cases from smoking?

Two separate, same-sized hypothetical groups — 1,000 people who never smoked, and a different 1,000 people who are current smokers — compared organ by organ.

These are population-level estimates, not a personal prediction. Each pair of bars compares two different groups of the same size, not the same people counted twice. Organs marked "approximate" in the references below use general-population lifetime risk rather than a never-smoker-specific rate, because that finer data wasn't available for every organ.

Methodology

  • Lung is the one organ here with directly measured absolute risk in both groups — a pooled European cohort/case-control estimate for continuing smokers, and never-smoker incidence data.
  • Every other organ uses a back-calculated never-smoker baseline: general-population lifetime risk, the current-smoker relative risk, and U.S. current-smoking prevalence (~11.6%, CDC/NHIS 2022) solved for the unexposed rate. This is more internally consistent than multiplying population-average risk directly by a never-smoker-relative RR, but it still assumes former smokers carry no excess risk, which isn't true — so it likely still understates the real gap between a genuine never-smoker and a current smoker, just less severely than the raw population-average approach would.
  • Relative risks are drawn from the most recent meta-analysis available for each organ at the time of writing (2018–2025); where only an older but still-authoritative pooled estimate exists (lung, larynx, esophagus), that's used and noted.
  • Numbers are rounded to whole people per 1,000 for readability; the exposed figure is calculated from the already-rounded baseline so the displayed ratio stays as close as possible to the cited relative risk.
