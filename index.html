A cancer development simulation

Run a lifetime of cell division, year by year.

Over a lifetime the human body goes through an enormous number of cell divisions to keep us alive. In this simplified example we will use the throwing of two dice to represent the process of cell division: most rolls are safe, but two disabling ones or "hits" in the same cell can turn it cancerous. Age advances like a clock, from 0 to 100. Each organ's line climbs at its own cell division rate, and small markers appear on a line the moment a replication error slips through uncaught. A second marker on the same organ means a cell has collected two hits and turned cancerous — after which that organ's line steepens, since cancer cells ignore normal growth control. Use the four controls below to test different scenarios.

Why this maps to real biology

Two hits, not one

Most cancers need more than one specific mutation to land in the same cell before it turns dangerous — a single hit usually does nothing on its own. This two-hit pattern is why each organ's first marker (amber) doesn't change its trajectory, but the second marker (red) does: that's the point a cell's growth control breaks down.

Why steeper lines matter

The number of stem-cell divisions a tissue accumulates over a lifetime correlates strongly with its cancer risk. Fast-turnover organs like colon and skin climb steeply and get more chances to roll badly — independent of any exposure — which is part of why they're diagnosed with cancer far more often than slow-turnover organs like heart or brain.

Why age accelerates, not just adds

Cancer incidence rises with age roughly as a power law, not a straight line — a pattern first described by the Armitage-Doll multistage model in 1954 and still used today. This model splits that into two separate mechanisms so they don't overlap: chronic inflammation (inflammaging) — from obesity, chronic disease, or aging itself — raises how often cells divide, while immunosenescence, the age-related decline in immune surveillance and repair capacity, is applied as a fixed decline that erodes whatever repair baseline you've set, for everyone, regardless of the inflammation slider.

Loaded dice

Inherited mutations like BRCA1/2 give every cell a head start — one hit already present at birth — so only one more unlucky roll is needed. Turn this on and watch cancer markers appear earlier and more often across organs.

Weighted dice

Smoking, cumulative UV, and heavy alcohol use all raise the odds of a bad roll on every division — but only once exposure has been underway for years, and the effect compounds the longer it continues. Push this slider up and the biggest difference shows up in later decades, not childhood, mirroring how smoking-related cancer risk depends far more on duration of exposure than daily intensity (Doll & Peto, 1978).

The referee

DNA repair enzymes and the immune system catch and clear most damaged cells before a second hit lands. This slider is the only control over that baseline capacity — turn it down and previously "safe" organs start accumulating markers too. Age still erodes whatever baseline you set, since immune function declines somewhat in nearly everyone over time.

This is a simplified teaching model, not a simulation of any specific cancer or organ. The relative division rates (0.03 for brain up to 1.00 for colon lining), the shape of the inflammaging/immunosenescence age curves, the mutagen exposure-duration curve, and the cancer growth multiplier are illustrative approximations that reflect documented directional patterns and published exponents where noted below — not measured, cancer-specific parameters for any individual or disease.

Real cancer risk depends on many more genes, tissue-specific biology, and individual variation than this two-hit, ten-organ model can capture. The goal here is intuition for how replication rate, mutagens, repair, aging, and inherited risk interact — not a risk calculator.

What this simulation is missing

  • Only two hits. Real cancers, especially common epithelial ones, typically need something closer to 4–6 cooperating mutations. The two-hit model here is a simplification for teachability, not a realistic mutation count.
  • No detection or screening. Real medicine catches and removes many precancerous changes (colonoscopy, mammograms, Pap smears) before they become cancer. This model has no "caught it early" mechanism — every hit that lands stays landed.
  • No infectious causes. A meaningful share of cancers worldwide are linked to viruses or bacteria (HPV→cervical, hepatitis B/C→liver, H. pylori→stomach). None of that is represented — the model only covers replication error, inflammation, mutagens, and inherited risk.
  • No hormonal or reproductive biology. Breast and prostate cancer risk are heavily shaped by lifetime hormone exposure (puberty, pregnancy, menopause, androgen levels). The model treats every organ like it has a flat division rate.
  • One cell lineage per organ, not a population. Real organs contain millions of independently dividing cells, any one of which could go wrong. Tracking one representative lineage per organ understates how many chances a real organ actually gets.
  • No tumor suppression after formation. Real immune systems sometimes recognize and eliminate early cancer cells even after a second hit (immune editing). Once an organ turns red here, it's permanent and just keeps growing.
  • Single run, not a distribution. One pass through the simulation is one possible life story, not a probability. Running it many times would give a truer sense of likelihood than any single pass.
