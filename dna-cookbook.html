Genetics, explained through the kitchen

Your DNA is basically a cookbook.

Every cell in your body carries a full copy of it. Let's zoom in, one layer at a time, from the whole collection down to a single typo.

Where it's kept

The cookbook lives in the kitchen.

Inside almost every cell is a nucleus — a safe, sealed room, like a kitchen where the family cookbook is kept so it never gets lost or damaged.

Inside the kitchen

You actually have two editions of every recipe.

Your cookbook isn't one set — it's two full sets bound together, one complete copy from your mother and one from your father. That means every single recipe inside exists in two editions, usually near-identical, sometimes with a real difference in the instructions. A different edition of the same recipe is called an allele.

The two sets bind together into 23 matching pairs, one pair highlighted — let's open it and compare editions.

Sometimes only one edition's instructions actually get followed when the dish is made — that's what "dominant" and "recessive" mean.

Opening one edition

Each set holds hundreds of recipes.

A single recipe inside is a gene — instructions for making one particular thing, like a pigment, an enzyme, or a hormone.

Reading the recipe

The recipe is written in a four-letter alphabet.

Zoom in far enough and a gene is just a long sequence of four letters — A, T, C and G — the actual DNA spelling out the instructions.

One letter got copied wrong — a mutation!

The finished dish

Follow the recipe, and out comes the dish.

A gene's instructions get followed to build a protein — the actual dish. What you can see and measure as a result, like eye color itself, is the trait.

Brown eyes — the dish that came out of this particular recipe, this particular edition, this particular volume, of your cookbook.
